Culture

Self-authorship, in community.

Identity, authority, and accountability aren't only things we build for agents. They're the things we build in ourselves — and in the organizations we make together. This page is about the culture the Dunathon is trying to grow.

On this page Three phases The platform problem Crypto, honestly AI, honestly The default world A culture set apart Authority, identity, accountability
01 · The arc

Three phases.

Self-authorship in community isn't a slogan. It's a direction of travel — from knowing yourself, to owning your choices, to showing up fully for other people.

1

Self

Find your footing and your own voice. Self-authorship begins with knowing what you actually think — not what you're supposed to think, not the answer that gets likes. What do you actually believe? That question is harder than it sounds, and more important than most of us realize.

2

Authorship

Author your own life. Own your name, your work, and your choices rather than renting them from a platform. Authorship isn't autonomy for its own sake — it's the precondition for making commitments that mean something, to yourself and to other people.

3

Community

Agency exercised together. Authorship isn't isolation — it's what lets you show up fully for other people. You can only give what you've actually got. When you know who you are and own what you do, you can be genuinely useful to people around you.

02 · The platform problem

The platform problem.

Most of us spend our days on platforms built for engagement — not for us. That distinction is worth holding on to.

What platforms optimize for

Reach without agency.

Platforms are tuned to hold attention. The documented costs show up as isolation, anxiety, and depression. You get distribution — sometimes a lot of it — but you don't own your audience, your data, or your relationship to the people on the other side. The rules can change tomorrow. Usually, they do.

What we're building toward

A place where what you do has weight.

Agency in community means being somewhere that you help govern — where you help set the rules, share in what's built, and answer for what you do. It's less about reach and more about standing. You know the people here. They know you.

03 · What we keep, what we leave

Crypto culture, honestly.

Crypto — which is shorthand here for blockchain-based finance and governance — produced some genuine ideas. Real ownership of digital assets. The right to exit a platform and take your things with you. The ability to build openly on each other's work, without asking permission. These are worth keeping. They're part of why a West Virginia DUNA (a Decentralized Unincorporated Nonprofit Association registered with the Secretary of State) is a real thing and not just a metaphor.

What we don't carry forward: airdrop farming, which is the practice of gaming systems for free tokens rather than building anything. Anonymity that shelters scams — and there have been a lot of them. Hype cycles that inflate the price of things before anyone has tested whether they work. The culture of extraction that sometimes passed for innovation.

We want the ownership without the extraction. We want the exit rights and the open building. We're less interested in making fast money and more interested in making things that last.

What's worth keeping — real ownership of what you make, the right to leave and take it with you, building openly on each other's work. What we leave behind — airdrop farming, anonymity that shelters scams, hype detached from anything real.

04 · What we keep, what we watch

AI culture, honestly.

The risks of AI culture are quiet ones. They don't announce themselves as risks. Deference — the habit of asking the machine what to think rather than thinking for yourself. Deskilling — the slow erosion of capabilities you no longer practice because it's easier to outsource them. Synthetic intimacy — the feeling of being known and understood by something that is, in a precise sense, making a very educated guess. Losing your own authorship to a tool that's happy to do the thinking, and the writing, and the deciding, if you let it.

None of these is inevitable. They're tendencies worth naming.

We treat AI as something that amplifies a person's authorship, not something that replaces it. An agent can do things faster, wider, cheaper than a human can — but a good agent answers to someone who knows what they're trying to do and why. The judgment has to live somewhere. We think it should live in you.

The risk worth naming — deference, deskilling, synthetic intimacy, losing your authorship to a tool that's glad to take it. We build agents that amplify the person, not ones that stand in for them.

05 · How we got here

Big Tech and the default world.

A handful of companies — you know their names — built the infrastructure most of us use every day. Search, maps, social networks, app stores, cloud storage, payments. They built it well. They also built it in a way that concentrates power at the center and turns the rest of us into data sources.

The result is what some people call the default world: one set of rules, one set of business models, one set of trade-offs — not chosen by most of the people living inside them, but ambient, like weather. You opt in the first time you make an account and mostly stop thinking about it after that.

We're not pretending there's an easy exit. Most of us will keep using these things. But naming the default world is the first step toward being able to choose, at least sometimes, something different.

06 · The harder task

A culture set apart.

Places like Esalen and Burning Man work — for the people who find them useful — because they're intentional and set apart from the default world for a while. The rules are different. The expectations are different. Something loosens. You meet people differently than you usually do. You might think about things you've been avoiding.

But the harder task has never been escaping the default world for a week or a weekend. It's carrying the values back. Returning to your ordinary life and finding that something has actually shifted — that you make different choices, relate to people differently, hold your own work with more care or more looseness, depending on which you needed.

The Dunathon is a ten-week version of that experiment. Long enough for habits to form. Short enough that the world it creates stays vivid rather than becoming just another ambient backdrop. We're building something set apart, with the explicit goal of taking what we learn back home.

The point isn't escape — it's practice. Ten weeks of doing things differently, on purpose, in a place where you know the people and the rules are ones you helped make. Then you take it with you.

07 · The same three things

Authority, identity, accountability — in people.

A DUNA gives an agent three things the open internet never could. It turns out those are the same three things a healthy community — and a well-built organization — gives a person.

01 · Identity

Full identity

Not a profile — a profile is a selection of things you're willing to show, optimized for an audience you're not sure you trust. A handle is a mask that's gotten comfortable.

In community — the whole person. Your history, your contradictions, your actual stakes. The kind of identity that accumulates weight over time because other people have seen you in more than one light.

02 · Authority

Self-authorship

Not a role — a role is something assigned, a part to play. It can be comfortable. It can also be a way of deferring the harder work of figuring out what you actually stand for.

In community — the standing to make your own choices and have them count. To author your own story rather than performing the one that's expected. To have your yes mean yes and your no mean no.

03 · Accountability

Answering for it

Not a rating — a rating is a number that aggregates strangers' impressions. It can be gamed. It doesn't require anyone to look you in the eye.

In community — answering for what you do, to people who know you. Not to an algorithm or a score. To the people whose judgment you respect, whose trust you've worked to earn, and would not want to lose.
